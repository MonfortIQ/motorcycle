Understanding Motorcycle Suspension Setup

Set rider sag first, then tune preload, compression and rebound around the motorcycle, rider weight and roads it will see.

Apex & Iron Workshop September 8, 2026 6 min read

Suspension tuning becomes much easier when each adjustment has a purpose. A measured baseline lets us separate spring support from damping control and make changes that can be felt on the road.

In this article

  1. 01Record a repeatable baseline
  2. 02Set sag for the rider
  3. 03Balance fork support and damping
  4. 04Tune the rear shock as a system
  5. 05Validate on familiar roads

Record a repeatable baseline

We begin with tire pressures, linkage condition and fork alignment, then record the current clicker positions. Worn bearings or a binding fork can make any tuning session misleading.

The rider’s normal gear, luggage and fuel load are included because they change the working load on the springs.

Set sag for the rider

Static sag measures how far the motorcycle settles under its own weight. Rider sag adds the rider and gear. These measurements show whether spring rate and preload are in a useful range.

Preload changes the starting position of the spring; it does not make the spring itself stiffer. If the correct sag cannot be reached within sensible adjustment, the spring rate may be wrong.

Balance fork support and damping

Fork spring rate carries load through the stroke. Compression damping controls how quickly the fork compresses, while rebound damping controls how quickly it extends after a bump.

We make small changes and note them. Large adjustments hide cause and effect, especially when fork oil condition or air gap is already outside the service range.

Tune the rear shock as a system

The shock works with the linkage ratio, spring and tire. We check for bottoming, wallowing and loss of traction instead of chasing a clicker number from another motorcycle.

A shock that is too stiff can skip across rough pavement; one with too little rebound control may pack down over repeated bumps.

Validate on familiar roads

A short route with a mix of smooth corners, braking and broken pavement makes comparison useful. We change one setting at a time and repeat the same section.

The final setup should remain predictable when the bike is hot, loaded and ridden at normal pace—not only feel firm in the workshop.

Workshop checklist

  • Write down every adjustment before turning a clicker.
  • Set tire pressure and mechanical condition before tuning.
  • Use the rider’s actual gear and regular load when measuring sag.
